01 · From bits to qubits

A classical bit is a switch

Every phone, laptop and server today runs on bits: each one is either 0 or 1. Flip the switch.

A bit is decisive but limited — it holds one answer at a time. What if a computer could work with a state that behaves differently from an ordinary 0 or 1?

02 · Enter the quantum world

The qubit lives on a sphere

A qubit's state is a point on a sphere. The north pole is 0, the south pole is 1 — and everywhere in between is superposition. Apply gates and watch it move; the bars show what a measurement would most likely give.

🌗

Superposition

Before measurement a qubit holds a weighted blend of 0 and 1 at once. n qubits track 2ⁿ possibilities simultaneously.

🔗

Entanglement

Two qubits can share one joint state, so measuring one instantly tells you about the other — stronger than any classical correlation.

📏

Measurement

Looking collapses the blend to a single classical 0 or 1, with probabilities set by the state. You only ever read out ordinary bits.

🌊

Interference

Amplitudes can add or cancel like waves. Good quantum algorithms are choreographies that cancel wrong answers and amplify right ones.

The key point: quantum computers aren't simply faster computers. They offer advantages for specific classes of problems — chemistry, certain optimization, factoring — while classical machines stay better at almost everything else.

05 · Why it matters

Two algorithms that changed the conversation

Grover's algorithm — searching the unsorted

Classical
Quantum

A quadratic speedup for certain search problems: work that scales like N for a classical machine scales like √N here. Real, but modest — it won't break encryption on its own.

Shor's algorithm — factoring large numbers

Classical
Quantum

An exponential speedup for factoring — the maths that much of today's public-key cryptography relies on. This is the main reason quantum computing matters to security. It needs a large, error-corrected machine we don't have yet.

06 · Will quantum break the internet?

The crypto question, in plain terms

Today's internet→ Public-key crypto (RSA/ECC)→ Large fault-tolerant quantum computer→ Some crypto breakable→ Post-quantum crypto

"Harvest now, decrypt later." An adversary can record encrypted traffic today and store it, betting that a future quantum computer will decrypt it. So long-lived secrets — health records, state files, banking, digital signatures — are already at some risk, even before a capable machine exists.

The defense is already standardized. In August 2024 NIST finalized its first post-quantum cryptography standards — FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) — and urged organizations to begin migrating.3,4 The US NSA's CNSA 2.0 sets 2030 as a migration deadline for national-security systems.4

This connects quantum computing to things you use every day: messaging, banking, cloud storage, government systems and digital signatures.

07 · The pairing everyone asks about

Quantum + AI

What researchers are trying

Quantum machine learning explores whether qubits and quantum circuits can represent or search certain patterns more efficiently than classical models — in chemistry, optimization and specialized kernels.

Where it may help

Simulating quantum systems (molecules, materials) is a natural fit, and some optimization sub-problems inside larger AI pipelines could benefit.

Current limitations

Loading big classical datasets into quantum states is expensive, hardware is small and noisy, and few quantum-ML advantages are proven at useful scale.

Why GPUs still win

Today's LLMs and neural nets run brilliantly on classical GPUs. Quantum is a research frontier for AI, not a replacement for the stack that trains models now.

Reality check: "quantum will make AI 1000× faster" is not an established result. Quantum machine learning is an active research area; treat production-scale claims with healthy skepticism.

The build-up

From qubit to quantum computer

Qubit→ Many qubits→ Quantum circuit→ QPU→ Error correction→ Fault-tolerant machine→ Quantum + classical hybrid

The hard step is error correction. Qubits are fragile, so a useful logical qubit is protected by many physical ones. Google's Willow chip (105 qubits, Dec 2024) was the landmark proof that adding more physical qubits can reduce the logical error rate rather than increase it — the threshold everyone was chasing.1,2
